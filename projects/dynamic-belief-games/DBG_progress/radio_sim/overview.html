Radio Signal Flow: Top-down Overview

Live Demo Top-down Overview

Where voice becomes “radio”

Your voice never “becomes RF.” A radio really performs two separate jobs:

  • Job 1 – Air → baseband: convert sound pressure into a clean low-frequency electrical signal (roughly 300–3000 Hz for voice).
  • Job 2 – Baseband → RF carrier: use that signal to modulate (control) a high-frequency electromagnetic carrier that the antenna can radiate. The receiver demodulates the carrier back into audio (or bits).

Transmitter (TX) innards

Everything that readies your voice for RF.

  • Mic element → preamp: air pressure becomes a tiny voltage that is boosted to a usable level.
  • High/low-pass filters: remove rumble and limit bandwidth so you stay inside your licensed channel.
  • Compressor/limiter: keeps loud speech from clipping later stages; FM transmitters often hard-limit because amplitude won’t matter at the receiver.
  • Pre-emphasis (FM voice): boosts high audio frequencies so the receiver can de-emphasize and reduce hiss.
  • Frequency synthesizer / LO: provides a rock-solid RF carrier (PLL locked to the channel).
  • Modulator: AM varies carrier amplitude, FM varies instantaneous frequency, and digital modulators map bits into phase/frequency/amplitude states (FSK/QPSK/QAM).
  • Driver → power amplifier: raises the RF envelope to the wattage you need.
  • Output filtering: cleans harmonics so you only radiate the assigned band.
  • T/R switch + antenna: routes the antenna to TX during PTT and launches the wave into space.

Receiver (RX) innards

Reverse the process to bring the audio back.

  • Antenna + preselector: captures microvolts and rejects out-of-band energy.
  • LNA: adds gain with minimal noise.
  • Mixer + LO: shifts the desired channel to an intermediate frequency (IF) or straight to baseband.
  • IF / channel filter: defines your 12.5 kHz (or similar) bandwidth and rejects adjacent chatter.
  • AGC: automatically rides gain so weak and strong signals both land in the codec’s sweet spot.
  • Demodulator: envelope detector for AM, discriminator/PLL for FM, clock recovery + demap for digital symbols.
  • De-emphasis + filtering: restores natural tone and reduces hiss.
  • Audio amp → speaker: drives the cone back into air pressure waves.

Analog vs digital mindset

Analog voice (AM/FM)

Continuous modulation—no packets, no framing. Your voice directly controls carrier amplitude or frequency.

  • Visualize with the RF waveform card in the live demo.
  • Noise shows up as hiss or fades; there’s no CRC or FEC to save you.

Digital / packet voice

Voice is sampled, quantized, compressed, framed, and then modulated as bits.

  • Packets/frames make sense only here.
  • Channel tricks: FEC, interleaving, CRC, jitter buffers.

Digital voice pipeline (voice → bits → RF → bits → voice)

The digital story is a repeatable eight-step loop. This is exactly what your demo animates.

  1. Mic + ADC: capture PCM samples (e.g., 8 kHz, 16-bit → 128 kbps raw).
  2. Frame slicer: cut the stream into 20 ms windows (160 samples at 8 kHz).
  3. Codec/vocoder: compress each frame to fewer bits (e.g., 8 kbps ⇒ 160 bits per 20 ms).
  4. Packetizer: group framesPerPacket codec frames into one payload and add header metadata (seq#, timestamp, length, type).
  5. Error protection: append CRC32 for detection and (optionally) add FEC + interleaving for correction.
  6. Modulator: map bits into RF symbols (FSK/QPSK/etc.) and generate the RF burst.
  7. Channel: delay, jitter, noise, fading, drops. This is where your sliders inject reality.
  8. Receiver chain: detect preamble/sync, demap symbols, deinterleave/FEC, CRC check, jitter buffer, codec decode, DAC → speaker.

What makes a “packet” in digital radio?

Many standards call them frames, but mentally you can treat them like packets. Each one has predictable fields:

Preamble
Known pattern so the receiver sees “a signal is here,” settles AGC, and aligns timing.
Sync word
A unique marker that says “frame starts now.”
Header
Version, type, addressing, color codes, slot info … whatever the protocol needs.
Payload
Codec bits (e.g., 20 ms of voice), optional signaling data.
Error protection
CRC for detection, FEC/interleaving for correction.

What’s in a packet, step by step?

Here’s the exact journey for one 20 ms frame of speech in this project’s demo. Change the frame size or codec and the math changes, but the structure stays the same.

  1. Sample & capture (20 ms @ 48 kHz): 960 audio samples enter the encoder.
  2. Codec compresses: µ-law = 960 bytes (7.68 kbit), PCM16 = 1920 bytes (15.36 kbit).
  3. Framer builds header: 12 bytes with version, codec flags, sequence number, timestamp, payload length.
  4. CRC-16: calculated across header+payload so the receiver can detect corruption.
  5. Packet conversion rate: 20 ms frames → 50 packets per second while PTT is down.
  6. Modulation mapping: payload bits are turned into AM/FM/BPSK symbols for display (or real radios use FSK/QPSK/etc.).
  7. Over the air: sender adds channel impairments (delay, jitter, loss). Receiver performs the inverse order: detect → CRC check → jitter buffer → decode → audio.
960 samples → codec bytes → header+CRC → modulation → RF channel → jitter buffer → speaker

Packet math & rate cheat sheet

Packets per second

  • Frames per second = 1 / frameDuration. With 20 ms frames → 50 fps.
  • Packets per second = fps / framesPerPacket. With 2 frames/packet → 25 pps.
  • Packets for a burst lasting T seconds ≈ ceil((T / frameDuration) / framesPerPacket).

Bit budget & overhead

  • Payload bits = framesPerPacket × codecBitrate × frameDuration.
  • Overhead bits = preamble (64) + sync (32) + header (96) + CRC (32) = 224 bits in this demo.
  • Total bits/packet = payload + overhead (×2 if FEC code rate = 1/2).
  • Required on-air bitrate = totalBitsPerPacket / (framesPerPacket × frameDuration) (≥13.6 kbps with the defaults).

Latency vs efficiency

  • More frames per packet → lower overhead %, higher latency, worse burst loss.
  • Fewer frames per packet → higher overhead %, lower latency, better resilience.
  • Show this in the live demo by adjusting “Frame size” and “frames per packet” (if you expose that control).

Rate waterfall (teach the conversion)

  • Raw PCM rate: e.g., 48 kHz × 16-bit mono = 768 kbps or 8 kHz ×16-bit = 128 kbps.
  • Codec rate: configured (e.g., 64 kbps µ-law, 8 kbps toy vocoder).
  • On-air rate: codec bits + overhead + FEC. Show all three so learners see “where the bits go.”

How many packets/frames when Andrew says “This is Andrew, how copy, over?”

Assume you’re using 20 ms codec frames (a common choice). That means 50 frames per second while PTT is down.

Codec frame = 20 ms Frames per second = 50 Duration = ~3 s speech
Total frames ≈ 50 × 3 = 150 Each frame → 1 RF packet (plus overhead)

Digital radios often glue multiple codec frames into a larger over-the-air frame, but this math keeps the story intuitive: “Hold PTT for 3 seconds? Expect roughly 150 packets marching through the live packet canvas.”
